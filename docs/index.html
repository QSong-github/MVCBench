MVCBench / Multimodal virtual cells / 2026

What can we really predict about drug-induced cellular phenotypes?

A systematic benchmark of molecular and gene representations across transcriptomic, morphological, and multimodal response prediction.

MVCBench disentangles representation choice, readout modality, and evaluation setting—so stronger benchmark scores can become more useful design evidence for virtual-cell models.

01 / SCIENTIFIC MOTIVATION

A virtual cell needs more than a strong representation benchmark.

Drug-response prediction is increasingly built from powerful pretrained encoders. But a representation that helps predict a cell image need not help predict gene expression, and success on familiar compounds need not persist across experimental settings.

The question is not simply which encoder wins. It is what information the encoder transfers, to which phenotype, and how much of that gain remains under the evaluation protocol.

01

What is encoded?

How much predictive information is contributed by a molecular or gene representation, under controlled downstream conditions?

02

What is predicted?

Do representation rankings change between transcriptomic responses and high-content image-derived morphology?

03

What transfers?

Which conclusions survive changing compounds, cellular contexts, plates, and datasets rather than relying on a single split?

02 / BENCHMARK DESIGN

One benchmark. Three linked questions.

MVCBench proceeds from component-level evaluation to multimodal optimization. Its datasets span transcriptomic and morphological responses, but observations from the two readout families do not generally originate from the same cells or matched experimental replicates.

Cross-modal matching is primarily at the compound level; interpret joint-modality evidence accordingly.

A / Chemical focus

Hold the prediction setting fixed; vary the compound representation.

Compare molecular fingerprints and learned chemical encoders as inputs to drug-response prediction. Evaluate transcriptional and morphological readouts separately instead of assuming one universal model ordering.

03–06 / EMPIRICAL EVIDENCE

The results are modality-dependent.

Interpret each result as evidence about its tested representations, datasets, readouts, and training protocol—not as a universal ordering of foundation models.

01 / Chemical input

Representation gains are stronger for morphology.

Learned and geometry-aware molecular features show clearer advantages in image-derived phenotype prediction than in gene-expression response prediction.

See evidence ↓
02 / Biological input

Task alignment matters for gene features.

Representations trained closer to perturbation response can outperform general-purpose alternatives in the tested downstream setting.

03 / Joint training

Integration must be measured, not assumed.

Joint objectives improve the studied benchmarks, but the magnitude and interpretation depend on modality pairing and optimization choices.

03 / MOLECULAR REPRESENTATIONS

A chemical encoder can be strong for images and ordinary for RNA.

Across the evaluated molecular representations, the benefit of learned chemical features is not uniform. For transcriptomics, improvements over classical fingerprints are often relatively modest. For morphology, several learned encoders provide more pronounced advantages.

This asymmetry motivates readout-specific benchmarking rather than selecting a single encoder from an aggregate score.

INTERPRETATION

Representation quality is task-conditional. Chemical information that helps recover structural phenotypes does not automatically explain high-dimensional transcriptional changes.

04 / GENE REPRESENTATIONS

Task relevance matters as much as pretraining scale.

MVCBench compares gene representation approaches, including single-cell foundation models, in a unified downstream evaluation. The reported results favor task-aligned features in several tested settings, with rankings changing by dataset and target subset.

This is a controlled comparison under shared downstream conditions, not a claim that every representation has been exhaustively optimized to its own best achievable performance.

WHAT THE COMPARISON SUPPORTS

Alignment with the target task helps.

The results motivate pretraining and representation objectives that match the biological response readout.

WHAT IT DOES NOT ESTABLISH

A universal foundation-model ranking.

Relative performance is contingent on datasets, held-out conditions, feature extraction, and the common downstream training budget.

05 / MULTIMODAL VIRTUAL CELLS

Does joint training provide more than two separate predictors?

MVCBench tests whether sharing an optimization objective across transcriptomic and morphological targets improves prediction relative to the studied single-task and auxiliary-training alternatives.

The reported improvements are evidence for the evaluated joint-learning setup. Because modalities are primarily linked by compound rather than matched cells, they are not direct proof of cell-level biological complementarity.

BASELINESingle-readout training

Independent objectives optimize transcriptional or morphological prediction.

COMPARISONCross-modal auxiliary training

One readout can be used as auxiliary supervision for the other.

EVALUATED APPROACHJoint multimodal optimization

Shared optimization is tested for benefits to both predicted response modalities.

06 / DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Build for the phenotype. Calibrate the comparison.

Design guidance should be conditioned on what was compared. MVCBench studies loss balancing, output-specific fusion, and the relationship between the two predicted response modalities.

01

Evaluate readouts separately.

Advanced molecular encoders show different relative gains for gene-expression and image-derived morphology targets.

02

Balance losses explicitly.

In the studied settings, calibrated fixed-ratio loss weighting performs competitively or favorably against the tested adaptive schemes; this should not be generalized without further tests.

03

Match fusion to the target.

Reported fusion preferences depend on the predicted readout and benchmark conditions; there is no evidence here for one universally optimal fusion method.

07 / OPEN RESOURCES

Reproduce the comparison. Test your own representations.

The source repository provides benchmark scripts, example representation-extraction notebooks, and links to the processed data. The page reports the experimental findings; the code documents what was actually implemented.
